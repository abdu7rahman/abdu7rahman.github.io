02

About

I write the software that decides where a robot goes next, and the bringup that gets it onto real hardware. Global planners, local controllers, reactive replanning for manipulators. Most of it started as someone else’s published method, checked against its own paper or a reference implementation, then fixed where it didn’t hold up.

Right now that means physical AI: policies for bimanual manipulation, from imitation learning to programs a model writes, and checking the simulators they run in against each other before believing a number. Before Northeastern it was five seasons with Team Robocon MJCET, building robots that had to work on a specific day in front of a judge, which is its own kind of benchmark.

  2. 02

    Pick and place that replans around what the camera sees

    UR12e and a Robotiq Hand-E. A RealSense depth pipeline with workspace filtering, robot self-masking and colour-based body exclusion isolates live obstacles, so when a hand enters the planned path the controller cancels cleanly, waits for the arm to settle and re-plans from the current state. Replayed against 90 recorded obstacle motions that all reach the arm, reacting at 0.2 m is no better than doing nothing, 2 of 90 against 0 (p = 0.50). Predicting where the obstacle will be and spreading the correction over every threatened waypoint gets 25 of 90 through untouched with the cube placed (p = 6 × 10−8).

  6. 06

    Robot foundation model

    Two models of its own on one action space. v1 is a VLA: a Molmo2-ER backbone with a flow-matching action expert, a latent world model and a competence head. v2 is Rolling-WAM, a world action model that denoises actions and imagined future frames together. Both carry the same embodied reasoner, a small language model tuned with GRPO on questions mined from the episodes, and an orchestration layer sits above them that never touches the weights. Every robot writes into one 32-D action space with a per-embodiment mask. Neither model has completed the task yet; v1 is 0 of 16, and the reasoner has not been trained to a result. What the repository has measured comes from its external baseline: a LoRA fine-tune of π0.5 lifts 93 of 100 in simulation where the released checkpoint manages 51, and a competence head on its features separates its failures at AUROC 0.932.

  7. 07

    Orchestrated bimanual assembly

    A pre-registered experiment that has not run yet. The question is narrow on purpose: does the robot foundation model’s instrument stack (latent world model, calibrated competence head, reasoner, Conductor and harness) earn its place around a LoRA-finetuned π0.5 on the NIST Assembly Task Board, two UR5s on a shared workspace, with the policy and the task held fixed. Built so far: the shape contract between the two stacks, scripted-expert controllers and detectors tested against a stand-in plant, and gates that order six phases, each opened by an artefact the CLI checks rather than by discipline. No gate is recorded and the Isaac Sim cell does not exist yet. The one measurement is a reason to freeze the vision tower: unfrozen, one optimiser step moved the world model’s target by 3.13, against 0.0 with a frozen copy.

  8. 08

    Quadruped navigation cost, and the constant that faked it

    Four cost functions for a Unitree Go2 (default Nav2 inflation, a hand-tuned legged costmap, a supervised learned cost and maximum-entropy IRL) sharing one planner, one controller and one 120-course suite so that only the cost function varies. The first table showed Nav2 losing decisively at 70.0%, with 20 of its 36 failures being slips on ice an occupancy costmap has no channel to represent. It came from FALL_RATE, a constant in my own simulator I had tuned by hand until the methods separated. A MuJoCo cross-check against the real Go2 MJCF found the drag sign backwards (ice is the slowest surface at 0.30, not the fastest at 0.95) and the robot no more likely to fall on ice than on grippy ground. With the invented hazard removed, Nav2 goes to 87.5% and the effect disappears. All four are now indistinguishable and every interval overlaps. Everyone building a robot-learning evaluation writes a surrogate like this; almost nobody validates it, and the null result is the evidence for doing so.

  9. 09

    One workcell, three simulators, and a false agreement

    Two UR5e arms over a shared bench, a Robotiq 2F-85 on each and four RealSense cameras. The cell is described once in xacro and emitted as MJCF, SDF and USD, so MuJoCo, Gazebo and Isaac Sim run the same robot instead of three that drift. cell-verify gates on 45 checks, 14 of them structural checks on the Isaac side. I had a section here claiming the two engines agreed on a pick-and-place task, six numbers within 2.8 %. They did not: both were scoring three objects lying still, and the tools only ever touched the bench. The descriptions match to 0.1 mm, MuJoCo now places 3 of 3 tools on the shipped seed and 3 of 10 episodes across ten seeds, and one tolerance, the at-rest speed, is validated against MuJoCo’s contacts while four stay provisional.

  10. 10

    Letting a model write the folding program

    Two ways for a model to write the policy. Gemini turns an instruction into Python against a small API (Code as Policies) or into a workflow graph (Graph-as-Policy); a sandbox runs it in the workcell above, the cell’s own primitives do every motion, and its detector judges the result. Each episode stores the exact program that drove it, and the ones that pass go out as LeRobot datasets on Hugging Face, private for now. On a long-sleeve top, programs fold 31 of 77 seeds and graphs 30 of 77, and both beat a hand-written order in pre-registered tests, 9 of 24 against 7 and 11 of 24 against 5. A stricter bar, LeHome’s checks plus a fold that lies flat and square like their teleoperated ones, only started passing once I looked at the geometry: the crease was standing on cloth-mesh edges 40 to 110 mm long. Split at 40 mm, one unedited program passes on 4 of 4 development seeds. The t-shirt and the pants do not yet, and all of it is simulated.

  11. 11

    Residual RL on a scripted handoff

    How much of a working but flawed controller’s failure can a small learned correction recover, without replacing it? A PPO residual on top of a frozen, scripted bimanual handoff controller takes success from 0.248 to between 0.852 and 0.996 across five seeds, 500 paired episodes each, in 400k steps where learning from scratch needed 800k to 1.2M. A reproduction of RECAP gains little from autonomous experience alone, 0.248 to 0.314, and reaches 0.910 to 0.950 only when an expert corrects half the episodes. All of it runs on a planar surrogate of the UR5e cell, not on hardware.

Measured

Claims I could check, checked, including the ones that came out badly.

My résumé claims porting the planners to C++ cut trajectory rollout latency by 2–3×. That was an estimate, so I wrote a harness that loads the real modules and times them on identical maps. The estimate was low, and it was low in a more interesting way than it was wrong.

Intel Xeon reporting 2.10 GHz · g++ 13.3 -O2 · Python 3.11, numpy 2.4. A shared host, so read the clock as a label: the machine before it ran these about half as fast, and the ratios moved by up to 40% between the two on identical work. Nothing is reimplemented: both harnesses call the planners' own code. Harness and method.
