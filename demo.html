Run my robots

Seven things on this page, and none of them are a recording. Four of them download the Python straight from reactive_autonomous_nav and reactive-replanning-ur12e and execute it here, unmodified — ROS is stubbed at the import boundary and nothing below it is touched. Search a map you draw; drive a TurtleBot with your cursor and throw obstacles in its way; race all five of the package's local controllers over one plan; then reach into a UR12e's workspace, block the arm, and watch it cancel and replan around your hand.

The last three come from repositories that are private, so their modules are copied into vendor/ rather than fetched — each with a PROVENANCE.md naming the commit and a SHA-256 per file. They still execute: walk a quadruped across the same course under two different cost functions and then break its physics on purpose; run an eight-phase bimanual assembly controller against its own analytic plant; and read a 32-dimensional action space and a nine-mode failure watchlist out of the model that defines them. The two families are listed apart below because the difference between fetching and copying is worth knowing before you read the byte counts in the console.

The whole stack, live

Chase

Your cursor is the goal. astar_planner.py plans to it, and dwa_controller.py tracks that plan — sampling a velocity window, rolling every candidate forward, scoring them on heading, clearance and speed, and committing to one, ten times a second at the node's own dt. Both files are the ones fetched above, running unmodified.

Click anywhere to drop an obstacle. The costmap is re-inflated, the global plan is thrown away and re-run, and the controller keeps tracking whatever comes back — which is the entire point of the repository.

Swap the controller and chase again. Same planner, same costmap, same cursor — only the thing following the plan changes, and they follow it differently enough to feel it. Each one draws what it is actually looking at: the point Pure Pursuit steers at, the point on the path nearest Stanley’s front axle — not its centre, which is the whole idea of the law — and the band TEB is deforming. Only DWA has a rollout fan, because only DWA scores a window of trajectories. MPPI is here too, but it runs in a worker: a tick costs about 580 ms and that is one indivisible call, so on the main thread it would freeze the cursor instead of chasing it. Off the main thread the page stays at 60 fps and the robot does what a real base does when its controller is slow — keeps moving on the last command until a new one arrives. Pick it and watch the lag in the readout; that number is the honest cost of 1000 samples a tick.

The last one is not from the repository — it was trained here. Clone is a small network fitted to DWA's own output: tools/train_clone.py drives the real dwa_controller.py across generated maps, records what it saw and what twist it committed to, and fits an MLP to that. Driving with it, the fan you see is the observation the network actually got — range along a spread of bearings, plus the goal in the robot's frame and its current speed. No velocity window, no rollouts: one forward pass. The section below has the numbers, including the ones that are not flattering.

Five controllers, one plan

Race

The package ships five local controllers. They all take the same job — here is a path, follow it — and they disagree about how. So: one A* plan, one costmap, five identical robots, and each controller running its own _control_loop at its own dt, on the tuning the repo ships. Nothing is normalised.

Fairness is in simulated time. Every frame advances each controller by however many of its own ticks add up to the same simulated interval, which is why MPPI takes two where the rest take one. Watch the two turns: a straight run would tie, and it is coming off the end of one wall and then the other that separates a controller which cuts the corner from one that holds the line.

MPPI is off by default, and not because it loses — it lands third of four on both maps of the package’s own controller suite, ahead of TEB and behind Pure Pursuit and Stanley. It draws 1000 samples a tick and spends real time doing it: about 470 ms a tick in this tab against roughly 2 ms for DWA, which is 18 seconds of driving that takes three minutes to watch. The profile puts that cost in _sample_controls and _path_angle_cost, not in marker building, so there is nothing to trim without running something other than what the repo ships. A robot with a real CPU makes its 20 Hz. Turn it on if you want to see it; the ms/tick column is why it is off.

Reach in

This is the UR12e cell from reactive-replanning-ur12e, seen down the RealSense. The arm runs the node's own cycle — open, PICK, close, PLACE, open, then park while the baseline rebuilds — and its joints are solved each frame rather than played back. The pick and place are not coordinates I picked: _build_poses() offsets the home pose by pick_z_offset and place_y_offset, and those two numbers are read out of the fetched source, so retuning the node retunes this. _cloud_cb runs on every frame of it: the depth gate, the workspace box, the colour mask that drops robot-coloured points, and the geometric self-filter that erases the arm from its own view. Every count on the right is read off the node's own filter calls.

Move your cursor to put a hand in the workspace. Put it on the dashed line the tool is following and the node does what it does on the robot: injects a collision sphere at SPHERE_RADIUS, notices a planned waypoint is inside SPHERE_RADIUS + PATH_CLEARANCE, cancels the motion, waits DECEL_WAIT for the arm to actually stop, and replans — lifting by DETOUR_HEIGHT and going over, or stepping aside, whichever plan_arc_detour picks for the direction it was travelling. Every threshold in that sentence is read off the node.

The interesting part is where it stops seeing you. Walk the hand onto a link and it vanishes into the self-filter — the repo's own test puts that edge at 11 cm, and you can find it yourself. Chase the tool and the dashed circle turns: that is PREEMPT_DIST, the radius inside which the node cancels the motion it is executing and replans. Both are the cost of never reporting the robot as an obstacle.

Cross the ice

A Unitree Go2 crossing a 12 × 12 m course from quadruped-learned-cost. Two cost functions get the same course, the same 13-channel observation, the same A* and the same DWA, and only the cost function differs: nav2_inflation is default Nav2 — an obstacle layer and an inflation layer, which is all occupancy can express — and reactive is a hand-tuned legged costmap. The third line is a privileged oracle handed the exact per-cell cost the simulator charges; it is a ceiling, not a competitor.

The course is course_suite(5, seed=1234), which is the benchmark's own suite, and the loop is qlc.eval.benchmark.run_episode, which is the benchmark's own loop: replan every replan_period ticks, DWA every tick, and a stuck counter so a stack that paints itself into a corner is recorded as a planning failure rather than as a timeout. Nothing here re-implements anything; the numbers under the plate come off the EpisodeResult.

Then break the physics on purpose. The switch puts FALL_RATE back to 0.5 and the ice drag back to 0.95 — the two constants the surrogate shipped with before a MuJoCo cross-check measured them. MuJoCo found the sign backwards (ice is the slowest surface at drag 0.30, not the fastest) and found no falls at all on flat low-friction ground, so FALL_RATE is now zero. That one constant produced 20 of the 36 failures in the Nav2 row of the first results table. Removing it moved Nav2 from 70.0% to 87.5% over 120 courses and the effect the project was built to show disappeared. Watch it happen on one course.

The two learned stacks are missing here and not by choice: qlc.cost.net is the supervised CNN and the MaxEnt IRL cost, both torch, and there is no wasm build of torch. The registry imports it lazily so the analytic stacks run without it. Their numbers — learned 86.7%, irl 81.7%, against nav2_inflation's 87.5% and reactive's 85.0%, every interval overlapping every other — are in the repository, measured on the same 120 courses. The comparison is a null result and the repository says so.

Seat a connector

Two UR5s on a NIST Assembly Task Board, from orchestrated-bimanual-assembly. What runs here is the controller: oba.sim.expert's eight-phase insertion machine and its five-phase bimanual wire-routing machine, scored by oba.sim.success — the same detectors the simulator arm uses — through oba.sim.rollout.rollout.

The environment under it is not a simulator, and this is the one thing to read before the plate. oba.sim.plant integrates commanded deltas, attaches an object when a gripper shuts near it, and develops a reaction force when a body is pressed past a surface. No friction, no compliance, no deformable wire mechanics. Its own docstring says it must never produce a reported number, and the repository enforces that with a type rather than a convention: IS_ANALYTIC is true, rollout refuses to record a phase gate when this is the environment, and PhaseGate.measured_by has no member that could describe it, so the mistake is rejected before a guard has to catch it. Every success rate the project reports is read from Isaac Sim. There are none on this page.

What is worth watching is the logic. ALIGN and INSERT are separate phases because a gripper-transition heuristic cannot tell them apart and the difference between them is the whole task. The socket's mating axis is published as its own frame rotated 180° about X, so a controller that assumes “insertion means descend” fails here rather than passing by luck. And the force band is ordered 1.5 < 8.0 < 30 < 45 N for a reason that cost a debugging session: with the back-off derived as a fraction of the maximum it landed below the force a good insertion produces, so every successful seat was aborted one step early and reported as NOT_GRASPED — a number pointing at the gripper for a fault in a threshold.

On wire routing, watch REGRASP. One link fills one clip, so pressing the same grip point into three clips in succession empties each as it fills the next. The controller has to let go and take hold further along. An earlier version did not, every episode ended with exactly one clip occupied, and the reported reason blamed tension.

This plant integrates translations only, so ArmCommand.ee_delta_rot_rad is ignored and the connector is published already at the mated orientation. The detector's angular tolerance is therefore not exercised by anything you see here — the repository states that in the module rather than letting the fixture look like it covers more than it does. It is covered by unit tests that build a misaligned state directly, and for real by Isaac Sim.

One action space, seven robots

No robot and no plate here: this is structure, and structure is the part of robot-foundation-model that is worth showing. Every embodiment in the mixture writes into one 32-dimensional vector, adapted from RDT-1B, and a per-embodiment mask decides which dimensions are meaningful. rfm.data.action_space.embodiment_mask builds that mask; the strip below is its output.

The reason to prefer one masked space over a head per robot is transfer — a bimanual episode teaches the shared representation something about single-arm reaching — and the cost is that masking bugs go silent. Compare single_arm_7dof with single_arm_osc_pos: the same physical arm under a Cartesian position controller writes four dimensions, not fourteen. Running it under the 7-DoF mask left ten dimensions supervised against a constant zero and diluted the gradient on the four that decide the task by about 3.5×. That is why the enum carries a separate member for a controller rather than for a robot.

The watchlist

Nine named failure modes, each with a metric, a threshold, a direction and a written consequence, live in rfm.eval.ablations.FAILURE_MODES. They exist because this project kept measuring healthy numbers over dead code paths — its own evidence index opens by counting twelve of them. Move a slider and rfm.eval.metrics.check_alarms scores the snapshot; what lights up is what that function returned.

The defaults trip nothing. Two of the nine are greyed out because they are scored by check_trace_alarms over a task's execution traces rather than by check_alarms over a metric snapshot, and no slider here can reach them. FM-2 is the one to push: the world model predicting z_t and calling it a prediction shows a dynamics cosine around 0.95 while the head carries no information, which is exactly the shape of the mistake the list was written to catch.
